"Come, come, children, sit closer to the fire," said Grandmother, and the
little ones crowded in, their eyes wide in the darkness. "You want to know
why Tortoise's shell is all bumpy and cracked? Then listen, and listen
well, for this is a true story, older than the baobab tree."

Long ago, in a village far away, at the edge of a great green forest, there
lived a Tortoise who was cleverer than all the other animals put together.
When Hare tried a trick, Tortoise saw it coming from a mile off. When
Monkey swung through the trees planning mischief, Tortoise was already
three steps ahead. But Tortoise had one terrible fault, and that fault was
greed. If there was food in the village, Tortoise wanted two plates. If
there was moonlight, Tortoise wanted two moons.

One dry season, a terrible hunger came to the village. The streams went
thin, and the yam mounds lay bare and dusty. All the animals were scared.
But Tortoise knew a secret. Deep in the forest, past the rocky hill, there
grew a magical garden. There, palms bent under the weight of the sweetest
fruits in the world, and from every branch hung a pot of honey, shining
like gold. Only one rule guarded the garden: whoever ate the food there
must share it, or the garden would close for ever and ever.

"Children, when Tortoise found the garden, did you think he remembered the
rule?" Grandmother asked.

"No, Grandmother!" the children shouted.

"No," Grandmother smiled. "He did not. Greed had covered his heart like
thick mud. He rubbed his hands together and laughed. 'Honey for every
little mouth in the village? Bah! I will think of something better.'

Tortoise called a meeting under the big baobab tree. Hare came, fast as
ever, hopping and wiggling his nose. Monkey came, swinging in from the
branches. Elephant came, slow and gentle, his great ears flapping like
sails. Lion came too, lying down with a great rumble of his belly,
pretending he was only there for the shade. And even Dog came, wagging
his tail, though Dog had only one tooth left in his mouth.

"Friends," said Tortoise in his smoothest voice, "I have found a garden of
honey and fruit. It is a long walk. I will go to fetch the food, and you
must wait here at the baobab and guard the fire."

"Will there be food enough for all of us?" asked Hare, licking his lips.

"Plenty," said Tortoise. "We shall all eat until we cannot move."

"Smart Tortoise," said Monkey, clapping his paws. "We are lucky to have
you."

And so it was that Tortoise walked on and on into the forest, and his
belly gurgled with impatience. When he reached the magical garden, the
air was thick with sweetness. He picked the first honey pot, bit the top
clean off, and drank until it was empty.

"One pot," he told himself. "Just one. Then I will carry the rest home."
But one pot made his mouth want ten pots, and ten pots made his feet want a
hundred. He ate. He ate. He ate. His belly grew round and tight as a drum,
and he burped out a long, loud "BRRRUMPP!"

At last, heavy and slow, he began the long walk home. Halfway there, his
eyes caught a spring of cold, clear water. "A drink first," he said, and he
slipped into the spring to cool himself. There he lay, belly up,
dripping, too full to move at all.

Now, what do you think? Who had followed Tortoise into the forest?

"Monkey and Dog!" the children cried.

"Monkey and Dog," Grandmother whispered. "Monkey, because Monkey suspects
everything, and Dog, because Dog smells what others cannot hide. Monkey
had been watching from the branches all along, and Dog had followed the
scent of honey all the way to the spring."

Monkey dropped out of the trees with a giggle, and Dog sat down, tail
wagging, eyes bright.

"So," said Monkey, "the clever Tortoise who promised us a feast has filled
his own belly with all of it!"

Dog barked, "WOOF, woof, woof!" which, if you remember, means "It is true,
and I am hungry too."

Tortoise pulled his head into his shell. "You cannot prove a thing," he
hissed. "Go home and wait at the baobab. I will bring the feast."

"Bring the feast, we will," said Monkey, twirling his tail. "And we will
call it the Feast of the Empty Pot."

And Monkey and Dog went home, and that very night, all the animals of the
village gathered under the baobab tree. Hare set a great clay pot in the
middle of the circle. Elephant lit the fire with a gentle puff of his
trunk. Dog carried a lantern. Monkey hung bananas from the branches like
lanterns too, so the whole village glowed warm and yellow.

When Tortoise limped into the village, too full and too slow to be
proud of anything, he saw them all. And there in the middle of the fire
light stood the great empty clay pot.

"Ah, my friends," said Tortoise, trying to look heroic. "I have brought
you the feast. The garden gave me every pot of honey, and I have poured
them all into this great pot for you to share. Alas, the pot is empty,
because, ahem, the wind must have blown the honey away."

Hare jumped up. "The wind?" said Hare. "You call that wind? I have seen a
stronger wind than you, and it only pushed Hare's tail sideways. Honey
does not fly, my friend. It stays where the little mouth puts it."

Monkey swung down and sniffed the air. "Honey does not fly either. But
I smell it. I smell it on you, Tortoise. I smell it on your nose, your
fingers, and your chin."

Elephant rumbled, "Child, a greedy mouth never shuts. It gapes open, and
the truth falls out."

And Dog did not need to say a word. He simply wagged his tail, barked
"WOOF, woof, woof," and lay down on Tortoise's left foot, right on top of
the honey sticky-sticky that had dripped there. The whole village laughed,
even Elephant laughed, and the baobab tree creaked as if it were laughing
too.

Then Tortoise stood very still. He had been clever all his life, but he had
never once been clever about honesty. The mud of greed was washing off his
heart, and for the first time, he could feel it underneath.

"I am sorry," said Tortoise, and his voice was small. "I stole the feast.
I should have shared it, and I did not. The garden's rule, I remember
now. Share, or the garden closes."

The animals looked at one another. Hare was angry. Monkey was furious.
But Elephant said slowly, "A pot emptied by greed stays empty. A pot
emptied by sharing fills again."

So the animals walked with Tortoise back into the forest. Tortoise knelt
by the magical garden and said, "I am sorry. I am sorry. I will share. I
promise." And the garden, which is kind when a heart is kind, opened its
arms. Pot after pot of golden honey, bunch after bunch of sweet fruit,
appeared in the circle of firelight.

Now, this is the part the children like best. Every animal ate until its
belly was round as a drum. Hare ate until he could only move in small,
fast hops. Monkey ate until he forgot how to swing. Elephant ate until he
could only walk sideways. Dog ate until he could not remember his own
name, and Tortoise, the proudest, greediest Tortoise of all, ate last, and
he ate only one little taste.

And when the feast was over, and the pots were clean and the fires were
low, Tortoise walked home with his belly light and his heart even lighter.
From that day to this, no Tortoise ever stole a feast, because Tortoise
remembered the day that a pot stood empty in the middle of the village,
and every single animal looked at him.

And that, children, is why Tortoise's shell is cracked. Not because the
honey was heavy. But because a greedy heart, once it cracks, never mends
quite the same again.

Grandmother wrapped her shawl tight and winked at the fire. "So do you
understand now? A clever head with a greedy heart is the foolishest head
in the whole wide world. Share what you have, and your pot will never be
empty. Keep everything for yourself, and the fire will go out."

"And remember the moral, all of you: greed turns the cleverest head into
a fool, and a stolen feast always ends in an empty pot."

The children were quiet for a long moment, listening to the crickets sing.
Then the smallest one whispered, "Grandmother? Tomorrow, will you make
honey cakes?"

And Grandmother laughed, and the fire popped, and the story went back to
sleep where all true stories sleep, in the keeping of the elders, to be
told again, and again, and again.
